💡 The Golden Rule of RVing: Take Your Time

Almost every rookie mistake happens when someone rushes. Slow down, communicate clearly with your copilot, work through your setup sequentially, and remember that fellow campers are always happy to lend a helping hand.

What Is Full Hookup Camping? (Water, Electric & Sewer Explained Simply)

When browsing campgrounds, you will frequently see the abbreviation "FHU." Having full hookup camping explained in simple terms means you have three individual utility hookups right at your parking space:

💧 1. Fresh Water Hookup

A municipal water spigot that feeds clean drinking water directly into your camper's plumbing. You get continuous water pressure without needing to fill your onboard tank or run your 12-volt water pump.

⚡ 2. Electrical Hookup (30 Amp or 50 Amp)

A shore power pedestal providing electricity to run air conditioning, lighting, the microwave, and appliances while recharging your onboard batteries without noisy generator exhaust.

🚽 3. On-Site Sewer Hookup

A capped sewer drain at your site. You connect a flexible drain hose to empty sink, shower, and toilet tanks directly into the system, avoiding communal dump station lines.

For anyone learning how to camp at an RV park, choosing full hookups gives you hotel-level convenience while enjoying the outdoors.

How to Connect Your RV at a Campsite (Step-by-Step)

Connecting your rig is straightforward when you follow a calm, repeatable arrival routine:

  1. Scout on Foot: Walk the site before driving in. Look up for low tree branches and check ground slope and utility locations.
  2. Position Your Camper: Pull or back into position, keeping utility hookups on your driver's side. Use a spotter with simple hand signals.
  3. Level Side-to-Side: Place leveling blocks in front of the low-side tires and pull up onto them until the bubble level centers.
  4. Chock Wheels & Unhitch: Firmly wedge chocks against both sides of the trailer tires before disconnecting from your tow vehicle.
  5. Level Front-to-Back & Stabilize: Adjust your tongue jack for level, then lower corner stabilizer jacks until snug. Never lift the trailer with stabilizers.
  6. Plug In Power: Ensure the pedestal breaker is switched OFF, plug in your surge protector and cord, and then switch the breaker ON.
  7. Connect Water & Sewer: Attach your brass water regulator and drinking hose, then attach your sewer hose with a sloped support track.

Power Management: 30 Amp vs. 50 Amp

Understanding electrical capacity prevents tripped breakers and appliance damage:

Feature 30-Amp System 50-Amp System
Plug Type 3 prongs (round/angled) 4 prongs (straight flat)
Max Wattage 120V • 3,600 Watts 120/240V • 12,000 Watts
A/C Capacity Runs 1 rooftop A/C unit Runs 2–3 A/C units simultaneously

A 50-amp service provides over three times the power of a 30-amp connection because it delivers two separate 50-amp legs. If you have a 30-amp rig, manage high-draw appliances wisely: avoid running your microwave or electric water heater at the exact moment the air conditioner compressor starts.

Water Hookup Tips & Water Pressure Regulators

Connecting water takes only minutes, but one safety rule is paramount: always use an RV water pressure regulator. Municipal water lines often deliver 70 to 100+ PSI, but RV internal PEX plumbing and plastic fittings are rated for 40 to 50 PSI. Screwing a brass regulator onto the campground faucet before your hose prevents blown fittings and costly interior water leaks.

Always use a certified drinking-water hose (white or blue) paired with an inline sediment filter. Regular garden hoses leach chemicals and leave drinking water smelling and tasting like rubber.

Leveling Your RV Correctly

Leveling your camper ensures comfortable sleep, proper drainage, and refrigerator safety. Absorption RV refrigerators rely on gravity-fed ammonia coolant; resting significantly off-level causes coolant to pool and permanently ruin cooling units.

Level side-to-side first with blocks under low tires while hitched, set wheel chocks firmly, unhitch, and then adjust the tongue jack for front-to-back leveling. Remember: stabilizer jacks only reduce frame sway—never use them to support the camper's weight.

Dumping Tanks (Explained Without Being Gross)

Handling wastewater sounds intimidating, but modern RV systems are completely closed and sanitary. Your camper holds two distinct waste tanks:

  • Grey Tank: Collects sink and shower water.
  • Black Tank: Collects toilet water and solids.

The Rule: Keep the Black Valve Closed! Even on full hookup sites, keep the black valve closed until the tank is two-thirds full. Leaving it open lets liquids drain away while solids harden on the tank bottom into a stubborn "pyramid plug."

To dump: put on nitrile gloves, attach your sewer hose with a clear elbow adapter, and pull the black valve first. Once empty, close it and pull the grey valve second. Soapy grey water naturally cleanses the hose. Finish by adding a couple gallons of clean water and an enzyme packet to the toilet.

RV Campground Etiquette: Being a Great Neighbor

Campground camaraderie relies on basic outdoor courtesy:

  • Quiet Hours: Respect park quiet hours (typically 10:00 PM to 7:00 or 8:00 AM) by turning down music and keeping late conversations soft.
  • Generator Rules: Avoid running loud generators during quiet periods. Full hookup sites eliminate the need for generators entirely.
  • Pet Courtesy: Keep dogs on a 6-foot leash, never leave barking pets unattended, and clean up waste immediately.
  • Site Boundaries: Never walk through another camper's site. Always stick to established roadways and paths.
  • Lighting: Extinguish bright exterior LED patio lights before heading to bed.

What to Pack for Your First RV Trip

This core checklist from our beginner RV camping guide keeps packing simple and practical:

  • Hookup Essentials: Heavy-duty RV electrical cord, 30/50 amp dogbone adapter, surge protector, drinking water hose, brass water regulator, inline filter, and sewer hose with clear elbow and support track.
  • Setup & Tools: Leveling blocks, wheel chocks, cordless drill with socket adapter for jacks, tire pressure gauge, hand tool set, and flashlights.
  • Sanitation: Disposable nitrile gloves, RV-safe quick-dissolving toilet paper, holding tank treatment pods, and clean-up supplies.

Choosing the Right Campsite for Beginners

Selecting the right site eases rookie stress. Pull-through sites let you drive straight in and out without reversing. Prioritize level pads with centralized utility pedestals and easy highway access so you never have to navigate tight obstacles with a trailer.

Common First-Timer Mistakes & How to Avoid Them

Steer clear of these frequent beginner missteps:

  • Forgetting Stabilizers: Driving off with stabilizer jacks deployed tears them from the chassis. Always walk around your rig before driving away.
  • Towing Full Tanks: Fresh water weighs 8.3 pounds per gallon. Carrying 50 unnecessary gallons adds 400+ pounds of fuel-draining weight. Travel light when heading to full hookup parks.
  • Ignoring Low Overpasses: Standard phone maps do not track bridge clearances. Use RV-specific routing and record your rig's exterior height.
  • Using Residential Toilet Paper: Standard paper does not dissolve in tanks, leading to clogs. Stick with RV-rated paper.
